# Natural en-US prose

Apply this module only to documents whose primary language is English. Produce en-US while preserving the document's genre, institution, purpose, and protected content. Treat naturalness as accountable communication, not as imitation of conversational speech or manipulation of authorship detectors.

## Set the language and genre contract

- Infer the document's macro-genre, section functions, intended readers, desired effect, and institutional constraints before judging its prose.
- Treat register as a bundle of choices shaped by situation, not as a scale from formal to conversational.
- Preserve differences among technical, institutional, legal, academic, literary, and informal prose.
- Normalize general prose to en-US unless a protected span or binding house rule requires another form.
- Follow a supplied house style when it conflicts with a nonessential default in this module.
- Preserve a stable point of view, level of formality, terminology policy, and relationship to the reader.
- Allow sections to modulate their register when their functions differ.
- Diagnose a feature only when it obstructs meaning, reader use, genre fit, stance, or deliberate effect.
- Revise only when the proposed form performs the passage's job better without changing its commitments.
- Use reader success, not a readability score or a presumed marker of humanness, as the test of clarity ([ISO 24495-1:2023](https://www.iso.org/standard/78907.html)).

## Shape information flow and reference

- Give new information an accessible attachment point.
- Begin from the active topic when continuity matters, then place the consequential or unfamiliar material where it receives emphasis.
- Treat old-to-new order as a strong English default, not as a fixed sentence template.
- Mark a genuine topic shift instead of disguising it with a generic transition.
- Reserve sentence endings for material that deserves stress unless suspense, meter, legal syntax, or another genre purpose calls for a different order.
- Place conditions, exceptions, negation, and scope where readers can connect them to the proposition they govern.
- Put a procedural condition before an instruction when late discovery could cause error.
- Keep a modifier close enough to its target to prevent a false attachment.
- Restore an explicit noun when a pronoun, demonstrative, or ellipsis has more than one plausible antecedent.
- Prefer a labeled reference such as “this result” or “that restriction” when bare “this” or “that” would make the reader reconstruct the referent.
- Repeat the established term when identity matters; do not cycle synonyms merely to avoid repetition.
- Use lexical variation only to mark a real distinction, prevent distracting echo, or achieve a deliberate literary effect.
- Preserve intentional ambiguity in literary or rhetorical passages; do not confuse purposeful openness with failed reference.
- Test adjacent sentences for both referential continuity and a recoverable logical relation.
- Test each section against the document's global topic and argument, not only against its immediate neighbors.
- Use direct antecedents where a bridge would impose needless work on the intended reader ([Haviland & Clark, 1974](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Haviland%2C%20S.E.%20_%20Clark%2C%20H.H.%20_What%27s%20new_%20Acquiring%20new%20information%20as%20a%20process%20in%20comprehension_%201974.pdf)).

## Control syntax and dependency load

- Diagnose structure before counting words.
- Inspect long gaps between a subject and its governing verb, a verb and its complement, a modifier and its head, or parallel elements.
- Inspect center embedding, nested qualifications, stacked prepositional phrases, delayed heads, and overloaded noun phrases.
- Move closely integrated elements nearer when distance creates a real processing burden.
- Split a sentence only when each resulting sentence retains the original logical relation, scope, and emphasis.
- Keep a longer sentence when it clearly holds together conditions, comparisons, qualifications, or a cumulative movement that belongs in one unit.
- Coordinate equal propositions and subordinate supporting ones so the grammar reflects their hierarchy.
- Use subordination to show cause, condition, concession, purpose, or evidence rather than presenting every proposition as an equal assertion.
- Convert a chain of clauses into a list only when the items are genuinely parallel and scanability serves the reader.
- Restore explicit subjects or verbs when excessive ellipsis makes a passage telegraphic.
- Remove duplicate syntactic framing when it creates monotony without rhetorical value.
- Retain deliberate syntactic parallelism when it clarifies comparison, builds emphasis, or establishes rhythm.
- Vary sentence form because discourse functions vary, not to meet a diversity target.
- Treat dependency distance as a diagnostic rather than a ban on long sentences ([Grodner & Gibson, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0000_7)).

## Choose agency, voice, and nominalization

- Name the actor when responsibility, authority, causation, or the next action depends on knowing who acts.
- Use active voice when it places the needed actor in topic position and clarifies action.
- Use passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, already established, deliberately withheld for a legitimate reason, or less important than the affected object, procedure, or result.
- Reject a passive construction when it obscures accountability that the reader needs.
- Preserve passives required by evidentiary caution, procedural focus, or an established disciplinary convention.
- Treat active and passive voice as information-structure choices, not quality labels ([Gopen & Swan, 1990](https://courses.washington.edu/b572/public/GopenSwan.pdf)).
- Keep a nominalization when it names a stable concept, defined process, legal category, measured construct, or useful topic across sentences.
- Unpack a nominalization when it hides the actor, action, timing, or causal relation.
- Break up long chains of abstract nouns when readers must infer how the concepts relate.
- Do not turn every noun into a verb if the resulting wording loses technical precision or terminological continuity.
- Use first person only when the genre and source authorize the writer to claim the action, judgment, or observation.
- Use second person only when direct address serves the reader and the genre permits it.
- Prefer an explicit institutional actor over a vague “we” when membership or responsibility is unclear.
- Preserve the distinction among the author, the institution, a cited source, participants, and the reader.

## Build paragraphs and mark relations

- Give each paragraph a discernible function in the section's work.
- Let paragraph shape follow function rather than enforcing a fixed number of sentences.
- Open with a clear orientation when readers need one; allow delayed focus when argument, narrative, or literary effect justifies it.
- Develop rather than merely restate the opening claim.
- Arrange sentences so evidence, example, qualification, comparison, and consequence attach to the claim they serve.
- End at the point of completed movement, not at an arbitrary length.
- Start a new paragraph when the controlling topic, rhetorical function, speaker, time frame, or argumentative step changes.
- Merge fragments that perform one inseparable function; separate blocks that conceal multiple jobs.
- Name causal, contrastive, conditional, temporal, or concessive relations when readers might otherwise misread them.
- Use a connector only when it labels the actual relation.
- Remove a connector that promises a relation the content does not deliver.
- Do not add a transition merely because a paragraph boundary looks bare.
- Prefer a direct topic sentence, repeated key term, or syntactic linkage when those devices create cleaner continuity than metadiscourse.
- Preserve productive inference for expert readers and literary audiences; supply missing bridges when stakes or reader knowledge require them.
- Check paragraph-to-paragraph movement separately from sentence-to-sentence cohesion.
- Check headings against the real hierarchy and purpose of the material they introduce.

## Calibrate stance and specificity

- Identify who is responsible for every material claim.
- Preserve whether a proposition is observed, measured, reported, inferred, estimated, recommended, disputed, or unknown.
- Keep evidential strength aligned with the supplied support.
- Preserve modals, ranges, limitations, populations, jurisdictions, conditions, and exceptions unless an approved substantive change resolves them.
- Never change “may” to “will,” “suggests” to “shows,” association to causation, or a qualified claim to a universal one for force or fluency.
- Remove ritual hedging only when it contributes no real epistemic or interpersonal function.
- Remove inflated certainty only when the source does not warrant it.
- Use hedges, boosters, self-reference, and reader engagement according to discipline and genre, not a universal voice recipe ([Hyland, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461445605050365)).
- Prefer a source-grounded actor, operation, date, quantity, example, or consequence when it lets the reader verify, distinguish, or act.
- Preserve abstraction when it carries the transferable principle or necessary technical category.
- Ground an abstraction with a representative instance only when the source supplies one or the text clearly marks it as hypothetical.
- Remove decorative precision that creates an impression of authority without helping the reader.
- Reuse one stable term for one stable concept.
- Define necessary specialist language at the level the intended reader requires.
- Do not replace a precise term with a familiar word unless the meaning remains equivalent.
- Never invent an anecdote, observation, quotation, citation, measurement, experience, opinion, or local detail to create authorial presence.

## Shape rhythm without manufacturing irregularity

- Make cadence follow thought, emphasis, pace, and attitude.
- Use a short sentence for a decision, pivot, warning, or release when brevity gives it force.
- Use a fuller sentence when conditions, qualifications, comparison, accumulation, or narrative movement belong together.
- Alternate neither lengths nor structures mechanically.
- Read difficult passages aloud to find accidental drag, breathless accumulation, weak emphasis, or repeated cadence.
- Keep the result recognizably written rather than imitating spontaneous speech.
- Use repetition deliberately for terminology, cohesion, insistence, pattern, or lyric effect.
- Remove repetition that merely restates a point without adding force, nuance, or orientation.
- Let punctuation articulate relationships and pace; do not use it as decorative texture.
- Retain a fragment, interruption, asymmetry, or abrupt transition only when it performs a clear genre-appropriate job.
- Preserve productive markedness in literary prose instead of regularizing every unusual construction.
- Avoid random fragments, forced asides, false starts, and deliberate errors.
- Ignore targets for average sentence length, “burstiness,” perplexity, or lexical unpredictability.

## Fit idiom and register to the genre

- Choose idiomatic en-US collocations without replacing exact domain language.
- Repair translation-shaped syntax or calques only when they are unintended and the revision preserves meaning.
- Avoid simulating a dialect, social identity, or regional voice that the source does not establish.
- Use contractions when they fit the relationship to the reader; do not add them automatically to sound natural.
- Avoid idioms, humor, and culture-bound metaphors in global technical prose unless they serve a deliberate purpose.
- Match technical prose to task completion, exact labels, explicit prerequisites, conditions, warnings, and results.
- Match institutional prose to accountable actors, neutral authority, traceability, and clear consequences without making it bureaucratic or chatty.
- Match legal prose to legal effect, stable defined terms, explicit scope, modality, conditions, exceptions, and jurisdiction.
- Match academic prose to the discipline's argument pattern, evidentiary practice, citation relations, and stance conventions.
- Match literary prose to point of view, sound, image, temporal control, implication, and intentional ambiguity.
- Match informal prose to the actual relationship and purpose; permit directness and contractions without fabricating slang or intimacy.
- Preserve a methods section's procedural density, an executive summary's decision focus, and an annex's reference function within one coherent document voice.
- Prefer familiar wording only when it conveys the same meaning, especially in legal and technical material ([UK Office of the Parliamentary Counsel, 2024](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/drafting-bills-for-parliament/2024-03-19-drafting-guidance)).

## Normalize en-US mechanics

- Use standard US spelling in unprotected general prose: for example, “color,” “behavior,” “organize,” “center,” and “labeled.”
- Apply US inflection and word forms consistently; do not alter proper names or quoted spellings.
- Use US vocabulary when an equivalent differs by variety, unless audience, domain, or official terminology requires the source form.
- Prefer unambiguous dates such as “August 13, 2026” in general prose and ISO 8601 dates in technical contexts that require them.
- Do not reinterpret an ambiguous numeric date; flag it instead.
- Use double quotation marks for primary quotations and single quotation marks for quotations within quotations unless the governing style requires otherwise.
- Place periods and commas inside closing quotation marks in ordinary US prose; preserve logical placement in code, literal strings, and styles that require it.
- Place semicolons and colons according to sentence logic rather than moving them inside quotation marks automatically.
- Use the serial comma when it prevents ambiguity or when the house style requires it; otherwise maintain the document's consistent policy.
- Use one space after terminal punctuation.
- Use em dashes, parentheses, colons, and semicolons according to function; do not ban or multiply any mark as a style signal.
- Close restrictive and nonrestrictive elements with punctuation that preserves the intended scope.
- Prefer “that” for restrictive clauses and “which” with commas for nonrestrictive clauses in edited formal prose, unless established usage or legal meaning calls for another construction.
- Keep capitalization, number treatment, abbreviations, headings, and citations consistent with the governing genre or house style.
- Preserve interface labels, commands, code, formulas, identifiers, file paths, and units exactly unless correction is explicitly in scope.

## Protect quoted and jurisdiction-specific language

- Treat direct quotations as verbatim spans.
- Preserve official names, titles, citations, defined terms, branded spellings, and jurisdiction-specific expressions.
- Preserve the original language variety inside quotations even when surrounding prose is normalized to en-US.
- Do not silently Americanize the title of a publication, law, institution, office, or cited work.
- Do not alter `must`, `shall`, `may`, `should`, negation, or defined terms in legal material for rhythm or variety.
- Do not repair grammar inside a quotation unless the user authorizes a marked correction.
- Keep `[sic]`, brackets, ellipses, and quotation omissions faithful to the source and citation style.
- Flag an apparent error in protected language instead of normalizing it silently.

## Avoid common overcorrections

- Do not shorten every sentence.
- Do not force active voice.
- Do not unpack every nominalization.
- Do not remove every hedge, transition, repetition, parallel structure, or abstract noun.
- Do not add a transition to every paragraph.
- Do not replace repeated technical terms with synonyms.
- Do not put the bottom line first when the genre depends on discovery, evidentiary sequence, suspense, or legal ordering.
- Do not make institutional or academic prose generically conversational.
- Do not make informal prose perform intimacy it has not earned.
- Do not introduce typos, slang, fragments, rhetorical questions, contractions, anecdotes, or personal pronouns as signs of humanity.
- Do not ban words, punctuation marks, sentence openings, or syntactic constructions because someone labels them “AI tells.”
- Do not maximize sentence variation, rare vocabulary, surprise, or any detector-facing statistic.
- Do not smooth away meaningful disagreement, uncertainty, asymmetry, or tonal restraint.
- Do not polish a weak argument into apparent authority.
- Do not change a proposition merely because another wording sounds more elegant.

## Run target-language acceptance checks

- Confirm that the text reads as idiomatic en-US for its actual genre, not as generic conversational English.
- Confirm that US spelling, inflection, vocabulary, dates, quotation practice, and punctuation are internally consistent.
- Confirm that every protected quotation, official name, defined term, legal modality, interface label, identifier, and jurisdiction-specific expression remains intact.
- Confirm that each sentence gives readers an accessible topic or a clearly marked shift.
- Confirm that every pronoun, demonstrative, ellipsis, and comparison has an unambiguous referent where ambiguity is not intentional.
- Confirm that long dependencies and embedded qualifications remain followable without destroying relations that belong together.
- Confirm that active voice, passive voice, and nominalization each serve information structure, accountability, or genre.
- Confirm that each paragraph has a recoverable function and a truthful relation to adjacent paragraphs.
- Confirm that each connector names the relation actually present and that no missing premise is being disguised as flow.
- Confirm that stance, attribution, modality, uncertainty, scope, causal force, quantities, and exceptions match the source.
- Confirm that every added detail is supplied, traceable, or explicitly hypothetical.
- Confirm that terminology remains stable and that lexical variation marks real distinctions.
- Confirm that sentence form and rhythm respond to meaning, emphasis, and pace rather than a numerical pattern.
- Confirm that the level of formality, directness, personal presence, and reader engagement fits the section's purpose.
- Read representative passages aloud and repair accidental stiffness, monotony, ambiguity, or breathlessness without making the document chatty.
- Compare the revision with the source after the language pass and reverse any semantic drift.
- Accept the revision only if it improves reader use or deliberate effect while preserving facts, reasoning, genre, and authorial commitments.
